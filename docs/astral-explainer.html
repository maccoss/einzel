How the Astral analyser works

An asymmetric-track multi-reflection time-of-flight analyser, reconstructed from four papers — including the design paper itself — and flown end to end. How the two mechanisms work, what the model reproduces of the published record, what it had to solve for, and what it still cannot claim.

1What the instrument is

The Astral is a time-of-flight mass analyser in which a 4 keV ion packet is reflected back and forth between two ion mirrors while slowly drifting sideways along them, so that about thirty metres of flight fit inside a box some 74 cm long. Each mirror is a pair of printed-circuit boards facing one another across a gap of about 41 mm, each board carrying four voltage strips and a grounded strip in front of them. Ions enter at a small angle to the mirror axis, zigzag outward for 24 oscillations, and then come back — the drift reverses on its own, with no deflector — to a detector beside where they came in. The papers report 24–26 oscillations, a flight time near 779 µs, and a resolving power above 100,000.A C

Two mechanisms make it work, and both are electrostatic. The mirrors are tilted toward each other by a few hundred microns over their length, which is what turns the drift round. And a shaped “ion foil” electrode — a stripe running along the drift between the mirrors — shapes the sideways potential so that ions entering with slightly different sideways speeds still arrive together.C D The model below carries both. It was built from nothing that is not in the four papers, and every number in it is either published, closed-form arithmetic, or measured by flying ions through solved fields. Where a number had to be solved for rather than read, this document says so; there are three of them.

3The mirrors

Each mirror is five electrodes on each of two boards. The strip nearest the drift is grounded; the next accelerates the ion into the mirror at −7.4 kV; the three behind it retard and reflect at +4.6, +3.9 and +5.9 kV, and the last of them wraps round to form the back wall of the mirror, so the whole far end sits at that potential. The electrode positions are published as drawn blocks in the design paper’s first figure,D and are read off it to half a millimetre. The voltages are published as multiples of the ion energy — −1.840, +1.158, +0.916, +1.503B — with two smaller correction vectors that trim the focusing in operation. The gap between the boards is published nowhere.

The design paper also plots the potential along the axis of the mirror, and that plot settles two things the table does not. The potential crosses zero directly beneath the second electrode, which it cannot do if that electrode is at −4.6 kV: the published table has the sign of the second voltage wrong, and both papers say in prose that electrodes two to four are positive.B D And the potential is still rising toward the reflecting voltage at the far end of the plot, which a flat strip with earth behind it cannot do either; the back wall does. With the sign corrected and the wall in place, the model’s axis matches the sixteen published points to 0.16 kV root-mean-square, and the axis was never used to fit anything.

What a mirror does, and how well this one does it

A time-of-flight mirror is a clock corrector. A faster ion covers the field-free drift sooner, so on its own the flight time falls with energy; but a faster ion also penetrates deeper into the mirror and spends longer there, so the mirror can be tuned to give exactly that time back. How well it does so is written as coefficients of the fractional energy offset δ:

T / T0 = 1 + c1δ + c2δ2 + c3δ3 + c4δ4 + …

and each coefficient that is cancelled buys one power of the energy spread in resolving power. The design paper states the mirror’s condition directly: the flight time is stationary at 4000 eV and again at ±100 eV either side.D Three stationary points fix three things — c1 = 0, c3 = 0, and c2 balanced against c4 — and there are exactly three numbers the papers do not give: the board gap, and the two reflecting voltages, which sit in a table with a sign wrong. So the three are solved for the three conditions, by Newton’s method on flown coefficients, and nothing else is adjusted.

The solution is a board gap of 41.4 mm, a third electrode at 0.974 times the ion energy against the table’s 0.916, and a fourth at 1.48 against 1.503. Repeating the solve with every electrode edge at its drawn position rather than a fitted one lands on the same three numbers, so the template carries the drawn layout and nothing fitted beyond the gap. The resolving power is stated as a range because a first-order residual of one part in ten thousand — the floor of what the solve, the mesh and a five-point fit can set, and a term the published curve itself carries — spans it.

The design paper publishes the slope of the flight time against energy, and it is the sharpest comparison available: the model’s curve has the same amplitude, ±0.034 against ±0.035 parts per million per electronvolt, and the same shape. The published curve’s zeros sit at 3958 and 4092 eV rather than at 3900, 4000 and 4100, which says the built instrument is tuned a shade off its stated condition, or that the printed plot is read with a small offset; the plot is not read finely enough to say which, and the difference is inside what the stripe and the detector set anyway. The effective mirror separation comes out at 646 mm on an on-axis half-oscillation and 640 on the full track, against a published 641.

Two things about that result are worth more than the number. The first is that the third-order term is controlled by the reflecting voltages and not by the electrode positions: moving the third and fourth electrodes’ edges over several millimetres could not touch it, while raising the third voltage by a fifth took it from 1.2 to 0.1. The second is that the back wall matters to the focusing and not only to the axis plot. A grounded boundary eleven millimetres behind a flat fourth electrode moved the third-order coefficient by 0.17 on its own — a grounded boundary is a third electrode, and where the real instrument has its reflecting potential the model had earth.

4Why the drift turns round

Nothing in the published drawings deflects the ions back. What does it is that the two mirrors are not quite parallel: a 200 µm spacerA tilts each board by a few hundred microradians, so the ions meet a surface inclined a little toward them at every reflection. The design paper gives the convergence as 0.045° and the injection angle as 1.78°.D

The effect has an exact closed form, and it does not depend on the electrode design at all. A structure that is uniform along a direction conserves the ion’s velocity component along that direction; reflecting off a mirror tilted by α therefore changes the sideways velocity by exactly

Δvz = V · sin 2α

per reflection, where V is the ion’s speed. The engine reproduces that to one part in 109, identically for a 20 kV/m and a 160 kV/m mirror. Forty-eight reflections of a constant decrement is a constant deceleration, so the drift is a parabola: the ion slows, stops at the reversal point, and comes back with the sideways speed it left with — the left-hand panel of the flight above. It follows that the number of reflections needed to reverse the drift is sin θ / sin 2α, which contains no length: a geometrically similar analyser of any size makes the same number of oscillations.

The design paper writes the slow sideways motion as motion in an effective potential, in units of the injected sideways energy, and splits it into the mirrors’ part and the stripe’s: the tilt supplies a straight ramp of 0.84 times the drift distance, and the stripe a fifth-order shape that dips through the middle of the drift and rises at its end.D The two sum to exactly one at the design length, which is what makes the ion turn round there; on the reproduced mirror the tilt alone would carry it to 404 mm, and the published stripe shape brings it back to 336. So the reversal is the tilt’s with the stripe’s shaping on top, about a sixth of the retarding potential at the turn coming from the stripe — which is the quantitative form of the papers’ two separate statements that the tilt reverses the drift and the foil corrects the aberration.C

A constant deceleration has a signature, and the model shows it cleanly when the stripe is switched off: vary the injection angle, and the return time is exactly proportional to the sideways speed (ratio 0.99 over ±5 per cent of angle). That proportionality is the whole problem in one number. Ions leave the source with a thermal spread of sideways speed of about 5.8 per cent at room temperature for m/z 500, and a return time proportional to that speed spreads their arrivals by the same fraction — a resolving power of about ten. The mirrors cannot touch this; they correct the along-track time, not the sideways one. Something else has to, and that is what the stripe is for.

5The stripe

The stripe is a pair of flat plates lying along the drift between the mirror strips, biased at a few volts to a few tens of volts.C Because the ion crosses the whole 41 mm gap between the boards tens of thousands of times a second, what its slow sideways motion feels is the stripe’s potential averaged across the zigzag — sampled on the mid-plane alone it looks negligible, and the ion is almost never there. The design paper gives the shape that average must have,D and this model expresses it as sixteen independently biased slices of a flat plate, fitted by least squares to the published shape minus what the tilt measurably provides on the reproduced mirror:

The fit reproduces the published shape to 0.23 per cent, and its sign is the published one: retarding through the middle, rising toward the exit. It also has to extend well past the drift it is designed for. Where the stripe ends its contribution decays, and that decay partly cancels the tilt’s continuing rise, leaving a crest followed by a dip; an ion with a little more sideways energy than nominal clears the crest and runs on to the tilt-only reversal far beyond. A first attempt made the stripe coextensive with the 350 mm drift and the turning point jumped from 339 to 409 mm for a 1.1 per cent change in injection angle. Extended to 450 mm the crest sits at 430, where it demands more sideways energy than any accepted ion has, and the requirement the design paper never states — because a correctly built instrument satisfies it without comment — is met.

Drift period and arrival time are two different quantities

Telling them apart is the whole of this section. The design paper gives an integral for each, and they differ in one place: the drift period is set by the sum of what the stripe and the tilt each contribute, and the arrival time by their difference.D A retarding electrode slows each bounce, while converging mirrors shorten the path and speed it up; the two mechanisms push the drift the same way and push the timing in opposite directions, and that opposition is what makes a cancellation possible at all. The flight time is then a whole number of bounces times the bounce period plus a small accumulated correction, and the paper requires every ion to make the same number of oscillations across the whole range of injection angles. The drift decides how many bounces happen; it does not decide when the ion lands.

A factor of more than fifteen hundred between two readings of the same flights, and both are right. The 47 is the number that had this work believing for weeks that the drift was hopeless; it was always an artefact of where the detector sat — a plane the ion crosses at whatever point in its bounce it happens to be in measures the drift period, and a fixed bounce count measures the arrival time. Sixteen segments are more than a hundred times worse than the published continuous shape and still support 73,500 from the drift alone across the instrument’s real acceptance. The range matters: measured across ±4 per cent of angle instead, the same quantity reads 340,000, because the timing error grows faster than linearly. Room-temperature thermal motion alone gives ±5.8 per cent at m/z 500, and the design paper accepts about ±11.

6Resolving power, and where the model stands

Four terms set the resolving power, and they are of two kinds. The mirror’s energy aberration and the drift’s timing error are properties of the shape of the field: dimensionless, unchanged by a change of size. The turn-around time in the extraction trap that feeds the analyser and the detector’s single-ion response are absolute times: fixed in nanoseconds, so their share of the budget is set by how long the flight is. At full scale and 786 µs the model’s mirror gives 120,000 to 220,000, its sixteen-segment stripe 73,500, a 1.04 ns turn-around from a rectilinear extraction trap at a 4 kV push 377,000, and the published 1.9 ns detector widthC 207,000. Combined in quadrature they give about 60,000 against a published figure above 100,000.

The gap is located to one term, and it is a representation rather than a mechanism: the stripe as sixteen biased segments. The design paper’s continuous shape holds the arrival time to parts per billion by quadrature, and even a modest fraction of that would lift the drift term out of the budget entirely, leaving the mirror and the detector as the co-limiters — which is where an instrument reporting above 100,000 with a 1.9 ns detector over a 786 µs flight has to be. What this model cannot claim is the last factor of two, and it knows why.

The two families behave oppositely under a change of scale, and that is the finding that carries into the compact design in the companion memo: a geometrically similar analyser at one-fifth scale keeps its mirror and its drift exactly and loses fivefold on turn-around and detector, so below about 40 per cent of full size it is the detector, not the ion optics, that sets what the instrument resolves.

7How this was learned

A reconstruction from four papers is a sequence of wrong turns corrected by measurement, and the wrong turns are recorded because each one is the kind a drawing invites. In the order they were made:

  • The tilt was invisible to the solver, and its sign was inverted. A convergence of 0.045° is a field anisotropy fourteen times below the discretisation error of any affordable mesh, so a volume solve of the tilted geometry returned 3.5, 0.011 and −0.57 of the closed form depending on the gap width. Rotating a two-dimensional cross-section instead is exact, and made each mirror a two-dimensional solve.
  • The tilt baseline was guessed at 250 mm and was 509. The instrument papers give only the spacer thickness; the design paper gives the angle. An injection angle inferred to make the reversal land compensated exactly, because the product of the two is what sets the reversal, so a register reproduced from the wrong pair of inputs looked right for weeks.
  • The drift period was measured and reported as the arrival time. A detector plane crossed at an arbitrary phase of the bounce reads the period; the paper’s isochronous quantity is the time at a fixed bounce count. A factor of 1.7 million between them, and an earlier revision of this document said the published precision was not reproduced when it was.
  • The electrode positions were guessed when they were published. Extracted text from the design paper carries no figures; the rendered page shows the electrodes as drawn blocks, the back wall, and an on-axis potential that fixes the sign of the second voltage. Reading a figure as a picture is a step, not an assumption.
  • A grounded boundary is a third electrode. Eleven millimetres of earth behind a flat fourth electrode moved the third-order coefficient by 0.17 and read the axis 1.15 kV low at the far end. The figure shows the electrode wrapping into the wall, and with the wall the solve for the three unknowns converged to the published curve.
  • The range a figure is measured over is part of the figure. The drift term read 340,000 over ±4 per cent of injection angle and 73,500 over the ±11 the instrument actually accepts, because the error grows faster than linearly. The narrow scan flattered the answer by a factor of 4.6.

8Established, assumed, open

Established

  • The drift reversal is set by the mirror tilt, with the stripe’s shaping on top — the design paper’s own account. The tilt alone reverses at 404 mm, the published stripe shape brings it to 336 against a published 335, and the closed form for the tilt’s impulse is exact.
  • Every geometric register number is reproduced on one flight: 336 mm reversal, 24 oscillations, 640 mm effective separation, 30.7 m, 786 µs.
  • The electrode layout is published, as drawn blocks in the design paper’s first figure, read to half a millimetre with both mirrors agreeing; electrode 4 wraps into the mirror’s back wall.
  • Electrode 2 is positive, from the published on-axis potential; the crowd-control table’s −1.158 is a sign error.
  • The published mirror is reproduced. With the drawn layout, the sign corrected and the three unpublished numbers solved for the paper’s three-point condition, the model gives 120,000–220,000 over ±2.5%, the published slope amplitude, and the published on-axis potential to 0.16 kV rms at sixteen points the solve never saw.
  • Drift period and arrival time are different quantities, and the published stripe shape is isochronous in the second: parts per billion by quadrature, and 73,500 flown with sixteen segments over the full acceptance.
  • Each cancelled focusing order buys one power of energy spread, measured as constant R·s and R·s² over 25-fold ranges.

Assumed

  • The board gap, U3 and U4 are solved rather than read: the gap is unpublished, and the two voltages sit 6 and 2 per cent from a table with one sign wrong. The on-axis potential cannot separate them; the focusing condition can, and the axis at the solution is closer to the published one than at any table value tried.
  • The gap between electrodes 0 and 1, 8.6 mm, is the width of the figure’s other small gaps and is what the on-axis potential across 150–230 mm chooses; the wall’s position and thickness are read off a drawing and the axis is indifferent to them.
  • The stripe as sixteen flat segments stands in for a continuous shaped electrode, and is what binds this model’s resolving power.
  • One ion, m/z 500, 4 keV throughout; the published figure is quoted at m/z 200, the demanding end.

Open

  • The published tuning against its stated condition: the digitised slope curve has its zeros near 3958 and 4092 eV and reads −0.026 at 4000, a first-order term the stated condition does not have. Not resolvable from the printed plot.
  • 24 or 25 oscillations: the count is set by the stripe amplitude, the instrument paper says 24 and the design paper’s tuning optimum is 25; this model runs at 24.
  • The continuous stripe: a shape function rather than segments would close most of the gap to the published resolving power, and is the next thing to build.
  • The fringe-field correctors the design paper mentions and this model omits.

9Sources and method

Everything here was derived from the open literature and the model's own flights. No proprietary drawings, no correspondence with the instrument's designers. The engine is Einzel, an open ion-optics platform; the mirrors are two-dimensional cross-section solves rotated by the tilt (exact, because rotations commute with the Laplacian), the foil is a three-dimensional volume solve, and every figure was rendered headlessly from a text specification beside the model, carrying its own provenance.
